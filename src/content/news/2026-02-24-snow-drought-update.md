## The Big Picture: Extreme Snow Drought Across the West

**Snow drought** occurs when winter snowpack is significantly below normal or melts earlier than expected, which drastically reduces our natural water storage. Unfortunately, extreme snow drought is exactly what we are seeing right now.

Due to an abnormally warm and dry winter, snowpack levels have remained exceptionally low across both the northern and southern Rocky Mountains. This will have significant hydrological impacts that will lag into the summer. Areas of extreme drought currently persist across the West, with portions of the central Rockies, northern Great Plains, and southern Plains experiencing extreme to exceptional drought conditions according to the US Drought Monitor.

*[PLACEHOLDER: Slide 11 - US Drought Monitor Map showing the widespread D3 Extreme and D4 Exceptional drought across the West]*

We are seeing daily temperature anomalies roughly 5 degrees above normal across most of the Western U.S. over the last 90 days, combined with precipitation hitting only the 10th to 30th percentiles [11-14]. This combination of heat and dryness leads to high evaporative demand (measured by the SPEI index), even during the winter months [14, 15].

## The State of Our Snowpack

When we look at Snow Water Equivalent (SWE)—the amount of liquid water actually contained within the snowpack—the picture is deeply concerning [16, 17]. While some high-elevation monitoring stations in the northern Rockies are showing near-normal snowpack, a massive amount of lower-elevation land that normally holds snow currently has zero percent of its normal snowpack [18-21].

*[PLACEHOLDER: Slide 38 - Snow Water Equivalent Percent NRCS 1991-2020 Median Map, highlighting the deep reds across the West]*

Looking at regional aggregates, we are seeing historically low conditions across multiple major basins:
*   **Upper Colorado River Region:** Currently at just 45% of normal [22, 23]. In fact, since our gridded snow data records began in 2003, this is the lowest snowpack on record for this time of year [23].
*   **Missouri River Region:** Sitting at roughly 42% of normal, and statistical models suggest we may have already hit our peak snowpack for the season [24, 25].
*   **Great Basin & Rio Grande Regions:** Sitting at 34% and 33% of normal, respectively, with very little expectation for significant additional accumulation [26-28].
*   **Pacific Northwest:** Currently faring slightly better at 74% of normal, but early-season atmospheric rivers actually degraded the snowpack by bringing warm rain that caused flooding instead of building snow reserves [29-31].

*[PLACEHOLDER: Slide 46 - Seasonal SWE for Upper Colorado Region chart showing the current trajectory tracking below the historical minimum]*

## Water Availability and Reservoir Impacts

Our snowpack is our historic storage for agricultural and potable water [32]. Because the snowpack is so depleted, we anticipate major reductions in the water coming downstream this spring [33, 34].

Normally, reservoir managers reduce pool levels during the winter to make room for a large pulse of snowmelt [35]. However, because there simply isn't as much water waiting up in the mountains, there is deep concern regarding our ability to recharge these reservoirs [35].

The Colorado River system is especially vulnerable right now [35, 36]. The total system contents are currently sitting at just 37% of capacity [36]. Lake Powell is at 26% of its full pool, and Lake Mead is at 34% [35, 36]. While some California reservoirs are sitting near or above normal, the broader Western water storage situation remains precarious going into a dry summer [35, 37].

## Climate Outlook: Transitioning ENSO Conditions

What is driving these patterns, and what comes next? We are currently transitioning out of moderate La Niña conditions [38]. The NOAA Climate Prediction Center forecasts that ENSO Neutral conditions will emerge as we move into the spring of 2026, with a high probability of transitioning into El Niño conditions by late summer [38-40].

Historically, the transition from La Niña to El Niño often correlates with the emergence of cascading aridity and drought, particularly starting in the southern Plains [41].

*[PLACEHOLDER: Slide 53 - Official NOAA CPC ENSO Probabilities chart showing the shift from La Niña to El Niño by late summer]*

Looking ahead to the next few months, seasonal outlooks suggest persistent warm and dry conditions will remain entrenched across the southern tier of the West [39, 42, 43]. While the northern Rockies and Pacific Northwest may see cooler, wetter conditions that could offer slight relief, the southern Rockies and southern Plains will likely see drought conditions persist and expand [39, 42, 44]. This dryness is also expected to drive above-normal wildland fire potential in the southern Plains heading into the spring [44-46].

## Preparing for the Season Ahead

The upshot is clear: communities and agricultural operations—especially those relying on irrigation—need to prepare now for lower-than-normal water availability this spring and summer [33].
